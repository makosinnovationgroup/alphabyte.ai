## Why Most Assessments Take Too Long {#why-most-assessments-take-too-long}

Most AI readiness assessment frameworks were built for enterprises. They assume a dedicated strategy team, a six-figure consulting budget, and three to six months of runway. The output is an 80-page deck that sits in a shared drive. By the time the recommendations are ready, the market has moved.

Mid-market operations leaders don't have that luxury. You're running a team, hitting quarterly targets, and trying to figure out where AI fits — all at the same time. You need a framework you can run in two weeks alongside your regular work. Not a research project. A diagnostic.

This is that framework. It won't tell you everything. It will tell you enough to make a decision: are we ready to move, or do we need to fix something first? That's the question that matters. The rest is detail.

The foundation of any AI readiness assessment is [data readiness](/services/data-readiness/). If the data isn't ready, nothing built on top of it will work. Week one addresses that directly.

## Week One: Data and Systems {#week-one-data-and-systems}

The first week is about understanding what you have. Not what you wish you had. Not what a vendor told you they could connect to. What actually exists today.

Start with an inventory. List every system that holds operational data. Your ERP, your CRM, your project management tools, your spreadsheets, your email inboxes. All of it. Most mid-market organizations have between five and 15 systems that matter. Some have more.

For each system, answer four questions:

- *Can you export the data?* Not theoretically. Can you actually get a CSV or connect an API today?
- *How current is the data?* Real-time, daily, weekly, or "whenever someone remembers to update it"?
- *What's the quality like?* Are records complete? Are fields consistent? Are there obvious duplicates or gaps?
- *Is the system connected to anything else?* Or is it an island?

Don't try to fix anything this week. Just document. You're building a map, not a plan. Most leaders are surprised by what they find — not because the situation is worse than expected, but because they've never seen all the data sources listed in one place.

If you want a deeper framework for evaluating data quality, our [Data Readiness](/services/data-readiness/) service uses a structured audit that scores each source on five dimensions. But for this self-assessment, the four questions above are enough to separate the viable from the problematic.

## Week Two: People and Workflows {#week-two-people-and-workflows}

Week two shifts focus from systems to humans. The goal is to map the human layer onto the data layer you documented in week one.

Start by identifying your most repetitive-task-heavy roles. Which team members spend hours each week on work that follows a predictable pattern? Data entry. Report generation. Invoice matching. Schedule coordination. Status updates across systems. These are the workflows where AI use cases tend to be highest-value and lowest-risk.

Next, find out who's already using AI. In most mid-market organizations, someone is. They're pasting data into ChatGPT to draft emails, using Copilot to write formulas, or running customer records through a free tool they found online. This isn't a problem to punish — it's a signal. It tells you where the pain is sharpest and where adoption will be fastest. Our [citizen developer enablement playbook](/blog/citizen-developer-enablement-playbook/) covers how to channel this energy productively.

Finally, assess comfort level. Not everyone needs to be an AI expert. But you need at least a few people on your team who are willing to test, give feedback, and iterate. If your entire team is resistant, your first project isn't an AI deployment. It's education.

> "Find out who's already using AI informally. That's not a problem to punish — it's a signal."

By the end of week two, you should have a clear picture: here's our data, here's where people spend their time on repeatable work, and here's how ready the team is to try something new.

## Scoring and Next Steps {#scoring-and-next-steps}

Score yourself on three dimensions, each on a scale of one to five.

*Data readiness.* A one means your data is scattered, inconsistent, and inaccessible. A five means your data is clean, connected, and exportable via API.

*Workflow complexity.* A one means your target workflows are highly variable with constant exceptions. A five means they're predictable, pattern-based, and well-documented.

*Team readiness.* A one means no AI experience and active resistance. A five means multiple team members already experimenting and open to change.

If you score three or higher on all three dimensions, you're ready to identify specific AI use cases and start building an AI roadmap. The question isn't *whether* to deploy, but *where* to start.

If one dimension is below three, that's your starting point. A low data readiness score means you need a data cleanup project before any AI work makes sense. A low workflow score means you need to simplify and document your processes first. A low team score means you need training and exposure before deploying anything.

This isn't a grade. It's a map. It tells you where you are and what comes next.

## When to Bring in Outside Help {#when-to-bring-in-outside-help}

The two-week assessment is designed to be self-directed. A VP of Operations or a Director of IT can run it without outside help. But there are situations where bringing in a partner makes sense.

If your data is fragmented across many systems with no integration layer, scoping the cleanup is hard to do from the inside. You're too close to the workarounds you've built over the years. An outside perspective helps identify what's essential and what's accumulated complexity.

If your team has no AI experience at all, the gap between "we scored ourselves" and "we know what to build" can be wide. A structured [Discovery](/services/discovery/) engagement compresses that gap. It takes the self-assessment output and turns it into a prioritized list of use cases with effort estimates and expected impact.

If you're in a regulated industry — healthcare, financial services, insurance — governance requirements add a layer of complexity. What data can go into an AI system? What decisions can AI inform versus make? These questions have compliance implications that are easier to navigate with someone who's done it before.

The point of the self-assessment isn't to answer every question. It's to answer the first one: are we ready, or what do we need to fix? Everything after that is a decision, not a mystery.
