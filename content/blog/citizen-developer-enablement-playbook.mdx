## What Citizen Development Meant Before Claude {#what-citizen-development-meant-before-claude}

The original citizen developer model promised that business users could build their own applications. The reality was narrower. You got Power Apps, Mendix, OutSystems — platforms that let non-technical people build forms, wire up simple logic, and connect to a limited set of data sources.

It worked for basic CRUD. Need a request form that writes to a SharePoint list? Done. Need an approval workflow with three steps? Covered.

But the moment a citizen developer needed to do something that touched real business logic — pulling data from a legacy ERP, writing conditional rules against complex datasets, building something that required more than drag-and-drop — they were back in IT's queue. The tools handled the simple cases. Everything else required a developer.

> "The tools handled the simple cases. Everything else required a developer."

The model had a ceiling, and most organizations hit it quickly. Citizen development became a label for form builders and basic automation. The hard work stayed where it always was.

## The Claude-Era Model {#the-claude-era-model}

The constraint was never the people. It was the interface. Business users understood their work deeply — they just couldn't express that understanding in a way that produced working software.

Claude changes the interface. An operations manager who can describe her weekly compliance review in plain English can now build a working tool that performs that review against live data. Not a form. Not a simple automation. A tool that reads documents, applies rules, and produces output she'd trust.

The architecture that makes this possible has three parts. Claude provides the reasoning and generation layer. MCP servers connect Claude to your actual systems — your CRM, your ERP, your file storage. And a governed environment with an [SDLC plugin](/blog/what-a-claude-sdlc-plugin-does/) ensures that everything built is auditable, versioned, and replicable.

This is citizen developer enablement with no code AI ceiling. The person doing the work describes the work. Claude builds the tool. Governance catches the mistakes.

The difference from the low-code era is not incremental. When you [start with the people who already know the work](/blog/start-with-people-not-process/), and you give them an interface that matches how they think — in natural language, against real data — the set of problems they can solve expands dramatically.

## The Playbook: Four Steps to a Governed Programme {#the-playbook}

Citizen developer programmes fail when they start with technology and hope adoption follows. They succeed when they start with the people who are already motivated and install infrastructure around them.

**Step one: find the people already using AI.** Every mid-market organization has them. Someone in operations who's been pasting data into Claude. Someone in finance who built a prompt template for monthly reporting. Find them. Learn what they built. That's your first proof point and your best signal for where to invest.

**Step two: install governance and guardrails.** Before you scale anything, the governed environment needs to be in place. The SDLC plugin enforces review. Permissions are scoped. Sensitive data is classified. Every project has a defined boundary. This is not bureaucracy — it's the infrastructure that lets you say yes to more people building more things.

**Step three: connect to real data via MCP.** The value of a citizen-built tool is directly proportional to the data it can access. Pre-wired MCP connections to your operational systems — your CRM, your ERP, your document stores — mean the first tool someone builds works against real data, not a sample dataset.

**Step four: create a graduation path.** A personal tool that proves useful becomes a team tool. A team tool that proves reliable graduates to a production asset with monitoring and support. This path needs to be explicit, documented, and supported by the [Citizen Development](/services/citizen-development/) infrastructure you've deployed.

Without the graduation path, you get a collection of personal scripts. With it, you get an organizational capability that compounds.

## Why Mid-Market Wins Here {#why-mid-market-wins-here}

Enterprise organizations have the budget for citizen development platforms. They also have the procurement cycles, the committee structures, and the integration requirements that turn a two-week deployment into an eight-month programme.

Mid-market is different. You have enough operational complexity to benefit — real workflows, real pain points, real data. You also have the organizational agility to move from decision to deployment in weeks, not quarters.

The [Sprinklermatic case study](/blog/sprinklermatic-nfpa-compliance/) is a clear example. A mid-market team with domain expertise and a specific compliance problem. The governed environment was deployed. The MCP connections were configured. The team was building against real NFPA data within the first two weeks.

That speed is not available at enterprise scale. And at small-business scale, the operational complexity rarely justifies the investment. Mid-market is the zone where the economics, the organizational structure, and the problem set all align.

The playbook is not complicated. Find the people. Install the governance. Connect the data. Build the graduation path. The hard part is not the technology. It's the decision to start.
